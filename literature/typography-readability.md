# Citation verification: typography and readability

Checked 2026-10-16 by a research agent. "Confirmed" = bibliographic record checked on a live page (DOI resolver/publisher, Crossref, Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar, PMC, repository or venue page). Usability News (Wichita State SURL) is an in-house newsletter, not peer-reviewed; flagged wherever used.

## Item 1 — Default body text size (16px / ~12pt)

**A. Rello, L., Pielot, M., & Marcos, M.-C. (2016).** Make it big! The effect of font size and line spacing on online readability. *CHI '16*, 3637–3648. https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858204
- Confirmed via Semantic Scholar API; findings via secondary summary (editingresearch.byu.edu/?p=10398). Peer-reviewed conference.
- Finding: 104 participants, 10–26 pt, eye tracking + comprehension. Objective and subjective readability and comprehension improved continuously up to 18 pt; authors recommend 18 pt or larger for text-heavy sites. Line spacing effects marginal.
- Implication: 16px is below the plateau; argues for a larger default or an easy override.

**B. Legge, G. E., & Bigelow, C. A. (2011).** Does print size matter for reading? *Journal of Vision, 11*(5), 8. https://doi.org/10.1167/11.5.8
- Confirmed via PMC3428264. Peer-reviewed review.
- Finding: fluent reading range ~0.2°–2° x-height; critical print size ~0.2° x-height (≈1.4 mm at 40 cm).
- Implication (agent arithmetic, flag for review): 16px with x-height ratio 0.45–0.5 at 50–60 cm ≈ 0.18–0.24°, right at the critical size. 16px is a floor; a low-x-height face (Garamond) pushes it toward the boundary.

**C. Bernard, M., Liao, C. H., & Mills, M. (2001).** The effects of font type and size on the legibility and reading time of online text by older adults. *CHI '01 EA*, 175–176. https://doi.org/10.1145/634067.634173
- Confirmed via Crossref. Extended abstract (lightly reviewed).
- Finding: older adults (mean 70) read 14 pt reliably better than 12 pt.
- Non-peer-reviewed companions (Usability News): 12 pt read faster than 10 pt; no font-efficiency differences at 12 pt.

## Item 2 — Line length (60–75 cpl)

**A. Dyson, M. C. (2004).** How physical text layout affects reading from screen. *Behaviour & Information Technology, 23*(6), 377–393. https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290410001715714
- Confirmed (full-text PDF read). Journal review.
- Finding: characters per line is the critical variable; speed often rises up to ~100 cpl but readers rate ~55 cpl easiest and dislike very long lines; print optimum ~52 cpl, Spencer ≤70.
- Implication: 60–75 cpl sits in the flat-speed, good-preference middle range. Evidence MIXED (speed vs preference).

**B. Dyson, M. C., & Haselgrove, M. (2001).** The influence of reading speed and line length on the effectiveness of reading from screen. *IJHCS, 54*(4), 585–612. https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.2001.0458
- Confirmed via Semantic Scholar. Journal.
- Finding: 55 cpl gave better comprehension than 100 cpl; 25 in between.

**C. Bernard, Fernandez & Hull (2002).** Usability News 4(2). NON-peer-reviewed. 45/76/132 cpl: no performance difference; adults preferred the two shorter lengths.

## Item 3 — Line spacing (1.5)

- **Rello et al. 2016** (above): spacing effects marginal; extremes worse; recommends default (~1.0–1.2×). Does NOT support 1.5 specifically.
- **Ling, J., & van Schaik, P. (2007).** The influence of line spacing and text alignment on visual search of web pages. *Displays, 28*(2), 60–67. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.displa.2007.04.003 — Confirmed (Teesside repository). Wider spacing improved search accuracy and speed; left-aligned beat justified.
- **Chaparro, Baker, Shaikh, Hull & Brady (2004).** Usability News 6(2). NON-peer-reviewed. Margins improved speed and comprehension; leading affected preference only.
- Verdict: 1.5 is "between single and double, preference-driven"; no study pins 1.5 as optimal. REVIEW.

## Item 4 — Serif vs sans; Garamond

**A. Arditi, A., & Cho, J. (2005).** Serifs and font legibility. *Vision Research, 45*(23), 2926–2933. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2005.06.013
- Confirmed via PMC4612630. Journal. No effect of serifs on reading speed; tiny threshold effects only near acuity limits.

**B. Ali, Wahid, Samsudin & Idris (2013).** *International Education Studies, 6*(3), 26. https://doi.org/10.5539/ies.v6n3p26 — Confirmed. Low-tier journal. No serif/sans readability difference.

**C. Josephson, S. (2008).** *Visual Communication Quarterly, 15*(1–2), 67–79. https://doi.org/10.1080/15551390801914595 — Bibliographic record confirmed; findings only via secondary (n=6, no overall serif/sans difference). Needs human check.

**D. Bernard, Mills, Peterson & Storrer (2001).** Usability News 3(2). NON-peer-reviewed. Garamond was NOT tested.

**E. Paterson, D. G., & Tinker, M. A. (1932).** Studies of typographical factors influencing speed of reading. X. Style of type face. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 16*(6), 605–613. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0070644
- Bibliographic record confirmed; findings via secondary only: Garamond fastest-read of ten print faces; common faces "equally legible". Numbers unverified against primary.
- NOT FOUND: any peer-reviewed study of a Garamond-style face for on-screen UI text at modern resolutions.

## Item 5 — Contrast polarity (light vs dark theme)

- **Buchner, A., & Baumgartner, N. (2007).** *Ergonomics, 50*(7), 1036–1063. https://doi.org/10.1080/00140130701306413 — Confirmed (PMID 17510822). Proofreading better with positive polarity in darkness and office light.
- **Piepenbrock, C., Mayr, S., Mund, I., & Buchner, A. (2013).** Positive display polarity is advantageous for both younger and older adults. *Ergonomics, 56*(7), 1116–1124. https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2013.790485 — Confirmed (PMID 23654206).
- **Piepenbrock, C., Mayr, S., & Buchner, A. (2014).** Smaller pupil size and better proofreading performance with positive than with negative polarity displays. *Ergonomics, 57*(11), 1670–1677. https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2014.948496 — Confirmed (PMID 25135324).
- **Dobres, J., Chahine, N., & Reimer, B. (2017).** *Applied Ergonomics, 60*, 68–73. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2016.11.001 — Confirmed (PMID 28166901). Negative polarity in dark ambient light was worst.
- Verdict: strong, consistent. Light theme default; dark theme as user preference.

## Item 6 — Syntax highlighting

- **Hannebauer, C., Hesenius, M., & Gruhn, V. (2018).** *Empirical Software Engineering, 23*(5), 2795–2828. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10664-017-9579-0 — Confirmed. n=390; no comprehension benefit.
- **Sarkar, A. (2015).** PPIG 2015, 49–58. https://ppig.org/papers/2015-ppig-26th-sarkar1/ — Confirmed. n=10; highlighting reduced task time, benefit shrinks with experience.
- **Beelders, T. R., & du Plessis, J.-P. L. (2016).** *Journal of Eye Movement Research, 9*(1). https://doi.org/10.16910/jemr.9.1.1 — Confirmed. No significant eye-movement difference; subjective preference for colour.
- Verdict: MIXED. Colour markup for preference, not a proven comprehension gain; keep subtle and switchable.

## Item 7 — Monospace vs proportional

- **Arditi, A., Knoblauch, K., & Grunwald, I. (1990).** Reading with fixed and variable character pitch. *JOSA A, 7*(10), 2011–2015. https://doi.org/10.1364/josaa.7.002011 — Confirmed (PMID 2231111). Proportional faster at medium/large sizes; fixed pitch better near acuity limit.
- **Mansfield, J. S., Legge, G. E., & Bane, M. C. (1996).** Psychophysics of reading XV. *IOVS, 37*(8), 1492–1501. PMID 8675391 — Confirmed. Times 5% faster than Courier for normal readers; Courier better acuity and smaller critical print size.
- NOT FOUND: monospace vs proportional for an EDITING task (alignment, tables). Human research needed.

## Item 8 — Blue as accent/text colour

- **Curcio et al. (1991).** *J. Comparative Neurology, 312*(4), 610–624. https://doi.org/10.1002/cne.903120411 — Confirmed. S-cones ≈7% of cones; S-cone-free zone ≈0.35° at foveal centre.
- **Williams, MacLeod & Hayhoe (1981).** Foveal tritanopia. *Vision Research, 21*(9), 1341–1356. https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(81)90241-8 — Bibliographic confirmed.
- **Gagl, B. (2016).** Blue hypertext is a good design decision. *PeerJ, 4*, e2467. https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.2467 — Confirmed (PMC5036113). No first-pass reading penalty for blue words; blue successfully draws attention.
- **Legge, G. E., & Rubin, G. S. (1986).** Psychophysics of reading IV. *JOSA A, 3*(1), 40–51. https://doi.org/10.1364/josaa.3.000040 — Confirmed. Wavelength rarely matters at photopic levels except near acuity limit.
- **Brettel, H., Viénot, F., & Mollon, J. D. (1997).** *JOSA A, 14*(10), 2647–2655. https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.14.002647 — Confirmed. Blue–yellow axis survives protan/deutan loss.
- **Viénot, F., Brettel, H., & Mollon, J. D. (1999).** *Color Research & Application, 24*(4), 243–252. https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1520-6378(199908)24:4<243::AID-COL5>3.0.CO;2-3 — Confirmed. Standard simulation matrices.
- **Ware, C. (2012).** *Information Visualization* (3rd ed.). Morgan Kaufmann. Textbook, not peer-reviewed; specific blue-text passage unverified.
- Verdict: blue accent/link text is fine with adequate luminance contrast; avoid tiny saturated blue on black. The "small blue text is illegible" claim comes from design guides, not peer-reviewed studies.

## Corrections and flags
1. Piepenbrock 2013 authors: Mayr, MUND, Buchner.
2. Bernard 2001 did not test Garamond; print evidence is Paterson & Tinker 1932 (secondary numbers).
3. No peer-reviewed Garamond-on-screen UI study.
4. No monospace-vs-proportional editing study.
5. No Piepenbrock 2014 "myopia" paper.
6. 1.5 line spacing: no study identifies it as optimal.
7. Rello 2016 spacing range inconsistent across secondary sources; check PDF.
8. Josephson 2008 and Hannebauer 2018 findings confirmed only via secondary pages.
9. Ware 2012 textbook claim unverified.
